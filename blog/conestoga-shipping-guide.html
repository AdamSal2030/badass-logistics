Conestoga Shipping: When the Rolling Tarp Beats a Flatbed or a Dry Van

Weather protection without losing crane access — and without a two-hour tarping job in the rain.

FIELD GUIDE BY THE CREW ✓
REAL LOADS — REAL NUMBERS NO FLUFF, JUST SPECS FROM THE DISPATCH DESK

Trailers · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 9, 2026

// quick answer

A Conestoga is a flatbed with a retractable tarp on a rolling frame. It gives you dry-van weather protection with flatbed loading access — crane from above, forklift from the side — and closes in minutes instead of a two-hour tarp job. You give up roughly 6 to 10 inches of cargo height and about 2,000 to 4,000 lbs of payload to the system.

A Conestoga solves one specific problem: freight that needs weather protection but cannot be loaded through the back doors of a dry van. Instead of tarping a flatbed by hand, you roll a curtain over the load on a frame.

How it actually works

The trailer is a flatbed underneath. Over it sits a tarp stretched on a rolling steel frame that retracts to one end like a concertina. Open, it is a flatbed — crane from directly overhead, forklift from either side. Closed, the load is sealed against rain, road spray, and dust.

Opening or closing takes one person a few minutes. Tarping an equivalent flatbed load properly takes two people the better part of an hour or two, in whatever weather is happening at the time. On a multi-stop run, that difference compounds fast.

What it costs you

Nothing is free. The frame and curtain take up space and weight:

Deck length48–53 ft

Payload~42,000–45,000 lb

So you lose roughly 6 to 10 inches of height to the frame and somewhere around 2,000 to 4,000 lbs of payload compared to a bare flatbed. If your load is right at the legal or weight ceiling, that matters. If it is not, you will never notice.

Conestoga vs the alternatives

  • vs flatbed: Same loading access, far better weather protection, no tarp crew. Costs more, and you give up a little height and payload.
  • vs dry van: Same weather protection, but you can load from the top and both sides instead of only through the rear doors. Critical for anything a forklift cannot roll in — machinery that has to be craned or slid on from the side.
  • vs tarped flatbed: Same protection on paper, but tarps chafe, pool water, and tear in transit. A curtain does not. On repeat lanes the labor savings alone usually pay the difference.

What about a double-drop Conestoga?

They exist, and they are genuinely rare. A double-drop Conestoga puts the rolling curtain over a lowered well, so you get weather protection for freight taller than a standard deck allows — roughly 10 feet inside the curtain instead of 8. Availability is thin and lead times are longer, so book early. For most tall covered freight, a double-drop with a purpose-built cover or shrink wrap moves sooner and costs less.

When to use one

  • Machinery, control panels, or electrical gear that cannot get wet but must be craned or side-loaded.
  • Palletized freight that would need tarping on every leg of a multi-stop run.
  • Finished or painted goods where tarp chafe would damage the surface.
  • Any lane where weather is unpredictable and tarping time is eating your schedule.

If you are weighing it against a tarped flatbed on a specific lane, send us the load and the route — the securement approach differs from a flatbed, and it changes the math. Or start with the trailer selector.

Frequently asked questions

What is Conestoga shipping?
Shipping on a flatbed fitted with a retractable tarp on a rolling frame. You load from the top or the sides like a flatbed, then roll the curtain closed for weather protection — no tarping crew required.
How much height do you lose on a Conestoga?
Roughly 6 to 10 inches compared to a bare flatbed, because the frame and curtain sit above the deck. Usable cargo height inside the curtain is typically about 8 feet on a standard deck.
Is a Conestoga better than a dry van?
For weather protection they are comparable. The Conestoga wins when the freight cannot be loaded through rear doors — anything that has to be craned from above or forklifted from the side. A dry van is usually cheaper and more available for standard palletized freight.
Does a Conestoga cost more than a flatbed?
Yes, typically a premium over a bare flatbed. But it removes the tarping labor and the tarp-damage risk, so on repeat lanes or multi-stop runs it often comes out ahead.
