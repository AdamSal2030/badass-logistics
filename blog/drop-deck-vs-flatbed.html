Drop Deck vs Flatbed: Which One Does Your Load Actually Need?

One number decides this, and it is not price. Here is how to pick without paying for deck you do not need.

FIELD GUIDE BY THE CREW ✓
REAL LOADS — REAL NUMBERS NO FLUFF, JUST SPECS FROM THE DISPATCH DESK

Trailers · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 9, 2026

// quick answer

Pick a flatbed if your load is under about 8 feet 6 inches tall — it is cheaper and more available. Pick a drop deck (step deck) if your load is between roughly 8'6" and 10 feet, because its lower deck sits about 19 inches closer to the ground and keeps you under the 13'6" legal height limit without a permit.

Freight brokers ask this backwards. They ask which trailer is cheaper. The right question is which trailer keeps your load legal, because a permit costs more than the trailer upgrade ever will.

The one number that decides it

Federal legal height on most routes is 13 feet 6 inches, pavement to the top of the load. Both trailers are 8'6" wide and both haul about 48,000 lbs. The only meaningful difference is how far off the ground the deck sits.

That is the whole comparison. Same width, same weight, roughly 19 inches of difference in deck height — and those 19 inches decide whether you file permits.

Pick a flatbed when

  • Your load is under 8 feet 6 inches tall.
  • You need continuous deck length — steel, pipe, lumber, long structural material that does not want a step in the middle.
  • You are loading from the side with a forklift and want the simplest possible approach.
  • You want the cheapest legal option and the widest pool of available trucks.

Pick a drop deck when

  • Your load is between about 8'6" and 10 feet tall — the exact band where a flatbed would push you into permit territory.
  • You are hauling mid-height machinery, forklifts, telehandlers, HVAC units, or crated equipment.
  • You want to avoid oversize permits, pilot cars, and daylight-only travel restrictions.

Note the trap: a drop deck's upper deck is the same height as a flatbed. Loading tall freight on the front section buys you nothing. The height advantage only exists on the lower deck.

What about "step deck"?

Same trailer. Drop deck and step deck are two names for the same equipment, and carriers use them interchangeably. A double drop is different — it has a lowered well between the axles and handles freight up to roughly 11 or 12 feet tall.

When neither one works

If your load is over about 10 feet tall, you are past what a drop deck can do legally. That is when you move to a double-drop or an RGN, which drops the well low enough to haul tall machinery and lets you drive equipment on from the front. Over 48,000 lbs and it becomes an axle-count problem — see multi-axle transport.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a drop deck and a flatbed?
Deck height. A flatbed deck sits about 60 inches off the ground; a drop deck lower deck sits about 41 inches. That 19-inch difference raises your legal cargo height from roughly 8 feet 6 inches to roughly 10 feet under the 13'6" limit.
Is a drop deck more expensive than a flatbed?
Usually a modest premium, because there are fewer of them and they are more specialized. But if a drop deck keeps you out of oversize permits, pilot cars, and daylight-only travel, it is almost always the cheaper move overall.
Is a drop deck the same as a step deck?
Yes — the same trailer under two names. A double drop is a different trailer, with a lowered well between the axles for freight up to about 11 or 12 feet tall.
How tall can a load be on a flatbed?
About 8 feet 6 inches before you need an oversize permit, working from the 13'6" federal legal limit minus roughly 5 feet of deck height.
